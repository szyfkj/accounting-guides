# Chart of accounts, journals, ledgers and the trial balance

These four tools turn a pile of receipts into organised records. Accounting software does most of the mechanics, but you should know what each one is.

## Chart of accounts

The chart of accounts is the numbered list of every account the business uses. A common numbering scheme groups accounts by type:

| Code range | Type | Example accounts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1xxxxx | Assets | 111000 Cash, 112000 Bank, 113000 Accounts receivable, 115000 Input VAT, 116000 Prepaid withholding tax |
| 2xxxxx | Liabilities | 211000 Accounts payable, 213000 Output VAT, 214000 Withholding tax payable, 215000 Social security payable |
| 3xxxxx | Equity | 311000 Share capital, 321000 Retained earnings |
| 4xxxxx | Income | 411000 Sales, 412000 Service income, 421000 Interest income |
| 5xxxxx | Expenses | 511000 Cost of goods sold, 521000 Salaries, 522000 Rent, 531000 Depreciation |

Keep the chart simple at first. Add an account only when you need to report that number separately.

## Journal

The journal is the book of original entry: each transaction in date order, with its debits, credits, a short description and the document number. Many businesses split it into a sales journal, purchase journal, cash receipts journal, cash payments journal and general journal.

## General ledger

The ledger groups all journal lines by account, so you can see the running balance of "Bank" or "Rent expense" at any date. Sub-ledgers hold the detail behind control accounts, such as one page per customer for accounts receivable.

## Trial balance

The trial balance lists every account with its closing debit or credit balance. Total debits must equal total credits. A balanced trial balance does not prove the books are right (an entry posted to the wrong account still balances), but an unbalanced one proves something is wrong.

## The accounting cycle

1. A business event happens and a document is issued or received.
2. The transaction is recorded in the journal.
3. Journal lines are posted to the ledger accounts.
4. Bank and other balances are reconciled to outside statements.
5. A trial balance is prepared and checked.
6. Period-end adjustments are made (accruals, prepayments, depreciation, inventory).
7. Financial statements are produced.
8. Income and expense accounts are closed to retained earnings, ready for the new year.
